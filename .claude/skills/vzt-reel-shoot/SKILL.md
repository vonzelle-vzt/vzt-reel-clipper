# Shooting for the edit

The edit can only ever be as good as the footage. Most reels that feel flat were
lost at the shoot, not the timeline — one angle, everything at the same frame
rate, no B-roll, autofocus hunting. This is what to capture so the footage cuts.

Use this **before** a shoot, and when diagnosing film that didn't cut well.

---

## 1. Camera movement

Most sports videography is following action, but there is still a lot of control
available.

**Game action — use a fluid-head tripod.** It is close to impossible to shoot
field sports handheld and get usable pans. A fluid head makes following the
action dramatically smoother. Mount it on a high hat or a tripod, whichever
suits the vantage point.

**Warm-ups are where you move.** This is the only time you aren't constrained by
following play, so it's where the energy clips come from:

- Orbit players while they stretch
- Move low-to-high and high-to-low
- Get in close with a wide lens
- Look for players **interacting with the camera** — that connection is what
  makes a viewer stop scrolling

Nobody wants a static shot of someone stretching. Keep the camera moving.

## 2. Angle variety

**The single most common failure is shooting the whole game from one spot.**
An edit cannot alternate angles if only one exists — and alternating angles is
what keeps a reel from feeling repetitive.

Move whenever you can: baseline → sideline → up into the stands. At college and
pro levels you're often locked to a sideline; get what variety you can within
that. Aim to be able to cut high → low → side.

**Give people a perspective the broadcast doesn't have.** Your audience can
already watch the highlights from the high wide angle. Your job is closeness and
energy — get near the players.

**Get low.** The "cowboy shot" from below does two things at once: it varies the
angle, and it makes the athlete look powerful.

## 3. Frame rates — vary them deliberately

Most shooters pick one frame rate and stay there all game. Don't.

| When | Shoot at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-ups / B-roll | **24 fps** | cinematic motion blur, plays back at real time without looking choppy |
| Game action | **60 or 120 fps** | headroom to slow down and speed-ramp in post |

Shooting warm-ups at 24 has a second benefit: it stops you leaning on slow
motion as a crutch and forces you to think about composition.

If you only ever hand over 30 fps footage, ramps have to synthesize frames
(`minterpolate`), which is never as clean as real ones.

## 4. Manual focus

Sports is unpredictable — someone walks through frame, a player leaves frame —
and autofocus hunting ruins a shot. A slightly-soft manual focus shot beats an
autofocus shot racking back and forth.

Manual focus also frees your composition: you're no longer tied to where the AF
point is, so you can use the rule of thirds and track much more easily.

**Ease into it.** Try it for a couple of plays next game, then more each time.
Use focus peaking, and an external monitor if you have one. Six months in you
will not go back.

## 5. Shoot the story, not just the highlights

A straight highlight tape is boring. The reel needs the human material, and it
has to be captured deliberately or it won't exist:

- Warm-ups and stretching
- Team huddles, pre-game speech
- Coach on the sideline
- Bench and crowd reactions
- Close-ups on faces — nerves, focus, joy
- **The ending**: handshake line, celebration, walk-off, trophy

Without these, the edit has no intro and no outro, and it will end abruptly.

## 6. Audio — assume you have none

Phone and DSLR film shot from the stands usually has no usable crowd mic. This
tool is built to work anyway (it scores on motion and semantics, not just
audio), but if you *can* capture decent ambient audio, contact and crowd noise
give the edit real texture.

Never rely on camera audio being loud enough to mark the big plays.

## Per-sport notes

The doctrine is universal; the positioning isn't.

- **Continuous flow** (basketball, soccer, hockey, lacrosse, volleyball) — play
  doesn't reset, so pick a spot with a long sightline and pan; get baseline and
  corner angles for variety.
- **Resets every play** (football, flag football, baseball, softball, wrestling,
  tennis, track) — you know when the next play starts. Use the reset to change
  position, and start rolling *before* the snap/pitch/gun so the setup is in the
  clip.
- **Track & field** — events are far apart; prioritize the start and the finish
  line, and use the gaps for B-roll.

## Pre-shoot checklist

- [ ] Fluid head packed and balanced
- [ ] Cards formatted, batteries charged, spares
- [ ] 24 fps set for warm-ups
- [ ] 60/120 fps ready for game action
- [ ] Manual focus practiced; peaking on
- [ ] A plan for at least two shooting positions
- [ ] Deliberate list of B-roll: warm-ups, huddle, coach, bench, crowd, faces
- [ ] Plan to stay for the ending — handshakes and celebration

## Handing film over

When submitting to this repo, note the sport, roughly where the action is, and
which files are which angle. Multi-angle and multi-file are supported —
see the `vzt-reel-ingest` skill.
